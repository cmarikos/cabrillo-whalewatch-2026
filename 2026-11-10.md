# Are California Brown Pelicans in Hot Water
#### Tammy Russel & Amelia DuVall - Ventura Audubon Society Webinar

##### [[California Brown Pelican]] - *Pelecanus occidentalis californicus*

![[Brown_pelican_in_flight_(Bodega_Bay).jpg|435]]
Habitat
- 5 major breeding subcolonies (So Cal Bight, Southern Baja Pacific, Gulf of California Salton Sea Extension, Gulf of California, Mexican Mainland, ...)
- Appear to function as a meta population - interchange likely occurs between colonies
- Breeding colonies on Santa Barbarda & Anacapa islands in the Channel Islands Nation Park
	- Only natural predator is the Island fox
- pyyawap'hew - Pelican Island, Chumash word for the channel islands

Breeding
- Increasingly asynchronous, nearly year round breeding season (this is not normal)
- Lay three eggs in a clutch - incubate 29-32 days
- Chicks fledge at 11-12 weeks

Previous Threat to Pelican Populations
- In the 1960s and 1970s pelicans suffered near total reporductive failure due to egg shell thinning - due to DDT
- Silent Spring - Rachel Carson help with DDT ban

Recovery
- pelicans have been de-listed as endangered since 2009
- There are still barrels of DDT sitting on the ocean floor

Monitoring
- Surveys
	- Of breeding colonies
		- Counting nests - captured with photos from above
		- Utilization of machine learning to more quickly and accurately count nests
	- At sea
		- Ship and aerial surveys
		- Many different short term surveys and long term programs in the [[California Channel Islands]]
	- Communal roosts
		- They congregate in communal roosts to rest
		- Counting strength among a certain age range
		- Looking for bands from previous rescues/rehabs
		- Looking for trash stuck on the birds
	- Tracking
		- Use UHF bands (e-bands) to track brown pelicans without impacting their behavior and survival - use solar charging and waterproof battery - bird who have been rehabilitated usually are candidates for being banded
			- Motus devices have unique radio ideas and each individual tower at [[MOTUS]] Sensor Stations picks it up
			- Data is publicly available: https://motus.org/explore/
		- Chicks have also been banded in their colonies - with collaboration of many local, regional and national groups

What have we learned so far?
- We previously thought, that after breeding, flocks move north. In early winter they return south to warmer waters. - National Park Service
	- What Motus has taught us is that many flocks do not follow this behavior
	- Some flocks don't migrate at all, a few observed flocks stay exclusively in the channel islands, moving around in the region only
	- Some flocks travel extremely long distances, and some do multiple migrations within one year. which also defies previous assumptions
	- For many flocks migration is not linear south to north, they bounce back and forth up and down the coast
	- This matters a lot, especially for the groups that migrate more frequently or longer distances - because migration takes energy in the form of food, so we can now understand which groups and how many will be impacted first when there are food shortages

Survival Analysis - when you get a ping from a bird, you know that bird is moving and still alive
	- Males and females have similar survival
	- Age categories have an impact - more hatchlings (< 1 year) die than any other age category
	- When the ocean conditions are warmer and less desirable for finding food, survivability does appear to decrease
	- Reproductive success is closely linked with the ability to forage fish - anchovy school size links with reproductions

[[El Niño]] Response
- Warm ocean temps can alter the abundance, and quality of available food
- If food is less available, they may abandon their nests/chicks, change distributions, and/or not survive
- Severe heat events can cause massive breeding failures and mortality events both as a result of food loss and of heat stroke to the birds themselves
	- El Niño and other [[Marine Heatwaves]] - both of which we've experiences this year have impacted the flocks 
- Three pronged warning
	- Long term climate change
	- Increased intensity, changing frequencies for El Niño events
	- Increased intensity and duration of other marine heatwaves

How can we help conserve populations?
- Hypotheses on current expected threats - we don't have good data on which of these matter most, though we suspect all of them play a role
	- Biological resource use
	- Invasive species, problematic species, genes and diseases
	- Climate change and sever weather
	- Pollution
	- Energy production and mining
	- Transportation and service corridors
	- Human intrusion and disturbances
- Experts asked the following then hypotheses are grouped in quadrants and highest priority quadrant are the most interest of to study
	- How uncertain are you about the threat
	- How much do we need to study this
	- How easy is it to study this
- Highest priority quadrant
	- Invasive plants in pelican breeding grounds - California Institute of Environmental Studies is doing environment restoration in piyawap'hew (anacapa Island) to return native plats to pelican breeding grounds and increase survival of chicks hatched on that island
	- Modus tower on east Anacapa Island

Sea temperatures are warming, but we're still not sure how this impacts this recovering species
- This past breeding season like the one before was very successful, where as last year has huge mortality 
	- What we're seeing is huge variability in survivability - and that is concerning for stability of the species population - but we still need longer term data to understand trends
	- We need to keep funding going to maintain long enough term data on this species

Life on land
- Different climates create different types of habitats on land
	- Coniferous forest
	- Mountain
	- Savanna
	- Polar ice
	- Tropical rainforest
	- Desert
	- Tundra
	- Mediterranean
	- Temperate grassland
	- Deciduous forests
- The age of the biome matters a lot, there are some forests that have been planted by humans that wildlife native to other similar biomes will not settle in

The coastal ecosystem
- [[Upwelling]] and salinity gradients create different hotspots for life
- There are dozens of different habitats in the [[California Bight]]
- Many whales travel in straighter lines right along the drop off of the coastal shelf - there is a lot of upwelling here which means more food
